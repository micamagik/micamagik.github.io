Which crystals you should never put in water

Water · Salt · Sunlight · Copper

Most advice about cleaning crystals treats them as one material. They aren't. A quartz point is chemically stable and effectively indestructible in water. Selenite will start dissolving. Pyrite will rust. Ethiopian opal will drink the water and go temporarily cloudy.

Here's what actually causes damage, sorted by the thing doing the damage.

Water

Three separate problems get lumped together under "don't get it wet", and they behave differently.

Stones that dissolve

Selenite, satin spar and desert rose are all gypsum — calcium sulfate — and gypsum is slightly soluble. Running water will dull a polished selenite surface, and prolonged soaking will visibly eat it. Halite, which is literally salt, dissolves outright. Dust these with a dry cloth and nothing else.

Stones that react

Pyrite oxidises in the presence of moisture and air, and it doesn't reverse. A wet pyrite cube can develop rust staining and, over time, crumble — a decay process collectors call pyrite disease. Malachite is a copper carbonate that water can dull and etch; it's also toxic in powdered form, so wet-sanding or prolonged soaking is a genuinely bad idea. Hematite can develop surface rust on lower-grade pieces.

Stones that absorb

Ethiopian Welo opal is hydrophane, meaning it's porous and drinks liquid. Submerge one and it can turn translucent and temporarily lose its play-of-colour. It usually recovers as it dries — over hours or days — but the same porosity means it will also absorb oils, soaps and dyes permanently. Keep opal away from water, lotion and perfume. Australian opal is generally not hydrophane, but it is sensitive to sudden temperature and humidity changes, which can cause fine surface cracking called crazing.

Safe in water

Quartz family stones — clear quartz, amethyst, citrine, smoky, rose quartz, agate, jasper, carnelian, tiger's eye — are hard and chemically stable. Warm water and a soft brush is fine. So are obsidian and most feldspars, including labradorite, though labradorite is softer and prefers a gentle touch.

Salt

Salt is the single most over-recommended cleaning method and one of the most destructive. Dry salt is abrasive enough to scratch softer stones and dull polish. Salt water is worse: it accelerates corrosion on anything metallic, pits softer minerals, and will wreck a wire wrap. If a piece is set in copper or silver, keep it out of salt entirely.

Sunlight

Colour in many stones comes from colour centres — structural defects that ultraviolet light can undo. The fading is permanent.

  • Amethyst fades toward grey or colourless in strong sun. This is the classic one, and a sunny windowsill will do it over months.
  • Rose quartz loses its pink.
  • Fluorite fades and is soft enough to scratch easily besides.
  • Celestite, kunzite and topaz all fade.
  • Smoky quartz is colour-centre based and will lighten.

If you want a stone in a window, pick something whose colour comes from its actual chemistry rather than a colour centre — most jaspers, agates and tiger's eye hold up fine.

Copper settings and wraps

A lot of talismans and tensor pieces are wound in bare copper, and copper tarnishes. That's oxidation, not damage — the metal is fine underneath, and plenty of people prefer the darkened patina.

If you'd rather have it bright: a soft cloth with a little lemon juice or a vinegar-and-salt paste will take tarnish off copper quickly, then rinse and dry thoroughly. The catch is that this method involves both acid and salt, so it's only safe if you can do it without touching the stone. On a wrapped piece where you can't isolate the metal, use a dry polishing cloth instead and accept a slower result.

A simple default

If you don't know what a stone is: dust it with a dry soft brush, keep it out of direct sun, and don't soak it. That routine is safe for everything and sufficient for almost everything.

Storage

Hardness is the thing people forget. Quartz sits around 7 on the Mohs scale and will scratch fluorite (4), calcite (3), selenite (2) and opal (5.5–6.5) without any effort. Tumbling loose stones together in one pouch is how polished pieces get scuffed. Wrap the softer ones separately, or give them their own compartment.
