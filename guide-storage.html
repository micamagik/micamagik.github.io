How to Store Crystals Without Ruining Them

Hardness pairing · Materials · Humidity · Labels

Most damage to a crystal collection is self-inflicted, and it happens in storage rather than in use. A bowl of tumbled stones on a shelf is an abrasion machine. A sealed box is sometimes worse than an open shelf. And the single most common loss is not breakage at all — it is forgetting what something is and where it came from.

Separate by hardness, not by looks

This is the rule that prevents most physical damage. Quartz sits at 7 on the Mohs scale and makes up the bulk of any collection. Everything below 7 gets scratched by it, and that includes a lot of the prettiest material you own: fluorite at 4, calcite at 3, selenite at 2, opal at 5.5 to 6.5, turquoise at 5 to 6, labradorite and moonstone at 6, apatite at 5.

Kept together and moved around, the quartz wins every time. In practice:

  • Anything at 6 or below gets wrapped or boxed individually.
  • Polished pieces never share a container with raw points — terminations act as chisels.
  • Selenite lives entirely on its own. At 2 it is softer than almost everything else, and it sheds fibres that get into other things.
  • Beads and small tumbles go in compartment trays rather than pouches, so they cannot grind against each other in transit.
Dust is quartz

Household dust is largely fine quartz particles. That is why wiping a soft polished stone with a dry cloth dulls it over time — you are dragging grit across a surface softer than the grit. For anything under 7, rinse or blow the dust off instead of wiping it dry.

Materials that help, and materials that harm

  • Good: unbleached cotton, acid-free tissue, plain cardboard specimen boxes, polyethylene or polypropylene containers, inert closed-cell foam.
  • Avoid PVC. Soft vinyl and some cheap plastic clamshells release chloride compounds as they age, which corrodes metal fittings and can etch susceptible minerals. If a container smells strongly of plastic, do not keep specimens in it.
  • Avoid open-cell foam and cheap sponge long-term — it degrades, holds moisture, and sheds into crevices.
  • Avoid wool and felt next to metal-set pieces; both can carry sulfur compounds that tarnish silver.
  • Cotton wool catches on raw terminations and leaves fibres behind. Tissue is better for anything spiky.

Humidity, in both directions

This is where sealed storage becomes a liability, because different minerals want opposite conditions.

Too damp and pyrite oxidises — the rust staining and eventual crumbling collectors call pyrite disease. Marcasite is worse. Any metal component tarnishes faster, and selenite slowly degrades. Pyrite in a sealed box with a silica gel packet is the standard answer, and the packet needs replacing or drying out periodically.

Too dry and opal suffers. Both Ethiopian and Australian opal contain water as part of their structure, and rapid drying is what causes crazing — a network of fine surface cracks that cannot be reversed. Do not store opal with desiccant, do not keep it next to a radiator, and do not leave it in a hot car. Stable and moderate beats dry.

The practical consequence is that pyrite and opal should not share a container. Storing a collection as one sealed unit optimised for one of them harms the other.

Light, weight and mechanics

  • Keep fade-prone stones in closed storage. Amethyst, rose quartz, fluorite, celestite and kunzite are better in a drawer than on a bright shelf. See what sunlight can do.
  • Do not stack heavy on delicate. Obvious, and still the most common way clusters lose their tips.
  • Support tall pieces. Towers and points stored upright without a base fall over eventually. A ring of foam or a proper stand costs nothing.
  • Wrapped pendants hang, not pile. Copper and silver wire deform under weight, and a wrap that loosens will eventually release the stone.

Label everything, immediately

This is the advice collectors give most often and beginners follow least. An unlabelled stone loses most of its interest and much of its value, because locality is a large part of what a specimen is. Six months on you will not remember whether the smoky quartz came from Brazil or Scotland, and you will not remember what you paid.

A slip of paper in the box with species, locality, date acquired, source and price is enough. If a piece came with any treatment disclosure, write that down too — it is exactly the detail that gets forgotten and exactly the detail that matters if you ever sell or trade.
