Stone hardness and what it means for you

The scale · What scratches what · Hardness vs toughness

The Mohs scale was put together in 1812 by the German mineralogist Friedrich Mohs, and it answers exactly one question: what scratches what. He picked ten reference minerals and ordered them so each one scratches everything below it. That is the whole system, and its simplicity is why it has outlasted two centuries of alternatives.

The scale

  • 1 · Talc — crushes under a fingernail
  • 2 · Gypsum — selenite and satin spar live here; a fingernail marks it
  • 3 · Calcite — a copper coin will scratch it
  • 4 · Fluorite
  • 5 · Apatite — window glass is about 5.5
  • 6 · Feldspar — labradorite, moonstone, sunstone, amazonite
  • 7 · Quartz — amethyst, citrine, agate, jasper, carnelian, tiger's eye, Herkimers; a steel file is about 6.5, so quartz scratches it
  • 8 · Topaz
  • 9 · Corundum — ruby and sapphire
  • 10 · Diamond
The scale is ordinal, not linear

The steps are not equal. Diamond is not "one more" than corundum — by absolute measurement it is several times harder, and the gap between 9 and 10 dwarfs the gap between 1 and 9. The scale tells you the order, not the distance.

Why 7 is the number that matters

Ordinary household dust is largely fine quartz. That single fact is the most practical consequence of the whole scale: anything softer than about 7 is slowly abraded by its own surroundings. Wiping dust off a polished fluorite sphere with a dry cloth is dragging quartz grit across a mineral three steps softer than the grit.

So for anything under 7, rinse the dust off or blow it away rather than wiping it dry. For anything at 7 and above, a cloth is fine.

Storage follows directly

The most common damage to a crystal collection is self-inflicted, and it comes from keeping everything in one bag or bowl. Quartz at 7 will scratch fluorite, calcite, selenite, opal, moonstone, labradorite and turquoise without any effort at all, simply by being moved around with them.

  • Wrap anything at 6 or below individually.
  • Never store polished pieces loose against raw points.
  • Selenite belongs on its own — at 2 it is softer than almost everything else you own, and it is also slightly water-soluble.
  • Opal at 5.5 to 6.5 scratches easily and, being hydrated silica, is sensitive to drying as well.

Hardness is not durability

This is the distinction that catches people out. Hardness is resistance to scratching. Toughness is resistance to breaking, and the two are independent properties.

Diamond is the hardest natural substance and will still cleave in two if struck along the right plane — cutters exploit this deliberately. Jade sits at only 6 to 6.5 but is exceptionally tough because of its interlocking fibrous structure, which is why it was carved into tools and axe heads for thousands of years while harder stones would have shattered.

Practical consequence: a hard stone is not automatically a safe stone to wear. Topaz at 8 has a cleavage plane and can split from a sharp knock. Quartz at 7 is far more forgiving of being dropped. If a stone has visible internal fractures, its hardness is close to irrelevant — the fracture is what will decide its fate.

Testing at home

You can approximate a hardness test with things you already own. A fingernail is about 2.5, a copper coin about 3.5, a steel knife blade or nail about 5.5, a glass plate about 5.5, a steel file about 6.5. Scratch in an inconspicuous place — the underside, or a hidden edge — and wipe the mark to check whether you actually cut the stone or just left a streak of the softer material behind.

The test is destructive by definition, which is why it belongs to identification rather than to curiosity. On a wrapped or polished piece, do not do it. It is far more useful for telling glass from quartz on a suspect tumble than for anything you already like.
