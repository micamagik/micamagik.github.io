How to tell a real Herkimer diamond

Identification · Inclusions · What to pay for

A Herkimer diamond is not a diamond. It is quartz — the same mineral as a common clear point — and the name comes from where it forms, not what it is. What makes it unusual is the shape: it grows doubly terminated, with a natural point at both ends, and it grows loose inside a cavity rather than attached to a rock face.

That combination is the whole story, and it's also why the name gets stretched. Plenty of double-terminated quartz on the market is sold as "Herkimer" when it came from somewhere else entirely.

What makes a Herkimer a Herkimer

They form in the Little Falls Dolostone of Herkimer County, New York — a dolomitic rock roughly 500 million years old. Silica-rich fluid moved through cavities in the stone, and quartz crystallised slowly inside those pockets with room on all sides. Nothing anchored the crystal, so it developed faces at both ends instead of growing outward from a base.

Two things follow from that, and both are useful when you're looking at a stone:

  • The faces are short and stubby. Herkimers tend toward a squat, chunky habit rather than the long slender prisms of most quartz points.
  • The surface is naturally bright. Because they grew free in a fluid-filled cavity, the faces come out of the ground with a glassy lustre. A genuine one does not need polishing, and most are sold exactly as they were found.
The quickest tell

Look at where the faces meet. On a natural crystal the edges between faces are crisp and slightly irregular, and the faces themselves may carry tiny growth steps or etch marks. On a cut-and-polished imitation the edges are too even, the facets are too regular, and you can sometimes catch fine parallel polishing scratches when you tilt it under a light.

The lookalikes worth knowing

Double-terminated quartz from elsewhere

This is the honest confusion. Balochistan in Pakistan produces double-terminated quartz that looks genuinely similar, and it's frequently sold as "Pakistani Herkimer" or, less honestly, just "Herkimer". It is real quartz and often quite beautiful — it simply isn't from Herkimer County. If provenance matters to you, ask directly where the stone was mined. A seller who hand-sources will know.

Cut and polished quartz

Ordinary quartz shaped into a double point and polished. This is the one to actually watch for, because it's sold at Herkimer prices. Check the edges and look for polish marks, as above. Also check the proportions: faceted imitations tend to be suspiciously symmetrical, while natural crystals are almost always a little lopsided.

Glass

Rare at any serious price point, but easy to rule out. Glass has no crystal faces to speak of, often contains perfectly round bubbles, and feels warmer to the touch than quartz because it conducts heat poorly. Quartz is also harder — around 7 on the Mohs scale — so it won't scratch the way glass will.

The inclusions are the interesting part

Herkimers are known for what's trapped inside them, and the inclusions are a good authenticity signal because they're difficult to fake convincingly.

  • Black flecks and patches are usually anthraxolite, a solid carbon-rich bitumen from organic material in the surrounding rock. It looks like a smudge of soot suspended in the crystal.
  • Water bubbles. A crystal with a visible pocket of trapped fluid is called an enhydro. If there's a gas bubble in that pocket you can sometimes watch it move as you tilt the stone. That water has been sealed in since the crystal formed.
  • Petroleum inclusions occur too, and some fluoresce under ultraviolet light — a bright blue-white glow from a stone that looks colourless in daylight.
  • Smoky and citrine tones come from natural irradiation and trace elements, the same way they do in quartz anywhere.
On the metaphysical claims

Herkimers are widely described in crystal traditions as amplifiers, dream stones, and attunement stones. Those are cultural and spiritual associations with a long history, not physical properties, and there's no scientific evidence for physical or medical effects. We list them here as tradition. Buy a stone because you like looking at it.

What actually drives the price

Once you've established a stone is genuine, price comes down to a fairly short list:

  1. Clarity. Water-clear commands more than cloudy — though a well-placed inclusion can be worth more than perfect clarity to a collector.
  2. Termination quality. Both points sharp and undamaged. Chipped tips drop the value quickly.
  3. Size. Price climbs steeply above roughly an inch, because larger intact crystals are much rarer.
  4. Something unusual. A moving enhydro bubble, a strong smoky phantom, or a dramatic anthraxolite inclusion is what turns a nice crystal into a collector piece.

A small, clean, well-terminated Herkimer is an inexpensive stone. Be suspicious of a large flawless one at a small price — that combination is exactly where the polished imitations live.

Caring for one

Quartz is hard and chemically stable, so a Herkimer is one of the easier stones to own. Warm water and a soft brush is enough. Avoid ultrasonic cleaners if the stone contains a visible enhydro pocket or a significant fracture, since vibration can worsen an existing flaw. Don't store it loose in a bag with softer stones — quartz will scratch most of them.
