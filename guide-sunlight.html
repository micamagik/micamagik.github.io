What Sunlight Can Do to Your Crystals

Colour centres · What fades · Safe display

Fading is the one form of crystal damage that is completely invisible while it is happening and completely irreversible once it has. A stone left in a bright window does not crack or crumble; it just quietly stops being the colour you bought it for, over a period long enough that you may not notice until you compare it to a photograph.

Where colour actually comes from

Two different mechanisms, and only one of them is vulnerable.

Some colour is chemical. The mineral contains an element that absorbs certain wavelengths — copper in malachite, iron in hematite, manganese in rhodonite. That colour is part of the compound and it is stable. You cannot bleach it out with light because there is nothing to undo.

Some colour is structural. It comes from a colour centre: a defect in the crystal lattice, often an atom knocked slightly out of place by natural radiation over geological time, which traps an electron and absorbs light as a result. Ultraviolet light supplies enough energy to release that electron and let the defect relax back to normal — and the colour goes with it. The stone is chemically unchanged. The structural quirk that made it purple has simply been undone.

Why this matters commercially

Colour-centre colour is exactly what heat and irradiation treatments manipulate. Heating amethyst to make citrine works by altering iron-related colour centres. The same mechanism that lets a treater create colour in a furnace lets sunlight remove it in a window.

What fades, and roughly how fast

  • Amethyst is the classic case and among the most sensitive. Strong direct sun will visibly grey it over weeks to months, and a south-facing windowsill will do it reliably. Deep saturated material has further to fall and shows it most.
  • Rose quartz loses its pink, ending up a cloudy near-white.
  • Fluorite fades in all its colours, and being soft at Mohs 4 it is vulnerable on two fronts at once.
  • Smoky quartz lightens toward clear, sometimes surprisingly quickly.
  • Celestite loses its pale blue readily.
  • Kunzite is notoriously fugitive — it was historically nicknamed an evening stone because collectors kept it out of daylight altogether.
  • Topaz, particularly treated blue and natural sherry-brown, fades.
  • Apatite, some beryls including aquamarine and morganite, and much dyed material of any species — dye pigments are often far less lightfast than mineral colour.

What holds up

Colour from chemistry rather than structure is stable in light. Jaspers, agates, carnelian, tiger's eye, obsidian, hematite, malachite, sodalite, lapis and most opaque banded material can sit in a bright room indefinitely. Clear quartz has no colour to lose. Labradorite and moonstone are safe too, because their effect is optical interference from internal layering rather than pigment.

Window glass is not protection

Ordinary window glass blocks most UVB but transmits a large proportion of UVA, and UVA is entirely capable of bleaching colour centres. "It is behind glass" is not a defence. Modern low-E and laminated glazing filters considerably more, but unless you know what you have, assume the light coming through your window is doing something.

Artificial light matters less than people fear. Incandescent and LED lighting emits very little ultraviolet, so a display cabinet under LED lighting is safe from fading — though strong spotlights close to a stone can still be a heat problem, particularly for opal.

Charging in sunlight

Leaving stones in the sun to charge them is a widespread practice, and for a jasper or an obsidian it is harmless. For amethyst, rose quartz, fluorite, celestite or kunzite it is the single most effective way to destroy the thing you paid for. If the practice matters to you, moonlight carries the tradition without the ultraviolet, and indirect daylight is a reasonable compromise.

Displaying vulnerable stones

  • Keep them off windowsills. A few feet back into the room reduces UV exposure dramatically — intensity falls off fast with distance and angle.
  • Rotate a display. If a piece must be in a bright spot, move it every few months so it does not fade unevenly. Uneven fading is more noticeable than uniform fading.
  • Use closed storage for the worst cases. Kunzite and celestite are genuinely better in a drawer, brought out to look at.
  • Photograph new pieces. A dated photo under consistent light is the only way to know whether something is fading, because the change is far too gradual to perceive from memory.
