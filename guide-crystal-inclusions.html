Inclusions: when the flaw is the point

Solid · Fluid · Phantom · Why they add value

An inclusion is anything trapped inside a crystal as it grew — another mineral, a bubble of gas, a pocket of liquid, a smear of organic carbon. In faceted gemstones inclusions are usually flaws that reduce value. In specimen crystals they are frequently the entire point, and often the most expensive thing about the stone.

The reason is straightforward. A clean crystal is beautiful but says little. An included crystal is a record of the conditions it formed in, and that record cannot be manufactured convincingly.

Solid inclusions

Another mineral that was already present, or growing at the same time, and got enclosed.

  • Rutile — golden or reddish needles of titanium dioxide, sometimes in dense sprays. Rutilated quartz is the best-known included stone there is.
  • Tourmaline — black rods running through clear quartz, usually straighter and thicker than rutile needles.
  • Chlorite — green, often in clouds or as a coating on internal growth surfaces, which is what produces "garden" or "lodolite" quartz.
  • Hematite and lepidocrocite — red to orange platelets, and the source of the pink in strawberry quartz.
  • Anthraxolite — solid carbon-rich bitumen, the black smudges characteristic of Herkimer diamonds, derived from organic matter in the surrounding rock.
  • Cacoxenite and goethite — brownish-gold and needle-like, among the seven minerals that define Super Seven.

Fluid inclusions

Sealed pockets of the liquid the crystal grew in. These are the ones that stop people in their tracks, because the water in them has been trapped since the stone formed.

  • Enhydro. A visible fluid-filled cavity. If a gas bubble is present as well you can watch it move as the stone is tilted — a two-phase inclusion, and the clearest possible evidence of natural origin.
  • Negative crystal. A void with the geometric shape of a crystal, sometimes fluid-filled. The cavity has the form the crystal would have had.
  • Petroleum. Hydrocarbon inclusions, found in Herkimers and in Pakistani quartz among others. Many fluoresce bright blue-white under ultraviolet light, which is a striking test on a stone that looks colourless in daylight.
Why inclusions are hard to fake

Surface features can be polished, colour can be dyed or irradiated, and shape can be cut. But a fluid inclusion sealed inside solid quartz, with a mobile bubble, cannot be introduced after the fact without leaving an obvious entry route. If you can see a bubble move, you are looking at a natural crystal.

Growth features that look like inclusions

Not everything inside a crystal is a foreign body. Some of it is the crystal's own history.

  • Phantoms. Growth stopped, a layer of some other mineral settled on the existing faces, then growth resumed and enclosed it. The result is the ghost of an earlier, smaller crystal visible inside the finished one — the same shape, suspended in place.
  • Veils and fractures. Internal healed breaks, often iridescent where a thin film catches the light. These are structural, and a large one is a genuine weakness.
  • Zoning. Bands of differing colour laid down as the fluid chemistry changed — amethyst commonly shows it, concentrated toward the tips.
  • Dendrites. Branching mineral growths on a plane inside the stone, so convincingly plant-like that dendritic agate is often mistaken for a fossil. It is manganese oxide, not a plant.

What inclusions do to value

They can move it in either direction, and the distinction is mostly about intent and placement:

  • Upward when the inclusion is distinctive, well-positioned and visually legible — a clean spray of rutile, a mobile enhydro bubble, a sharply defined phantom. This is where specimen pricing diverges most from gem pricing.
  • Downward when it is general haze, dense cloudiness with no structure, or a large fracture that compromises the stone.

A useful way to judge: does the inclusion give you something to look at, or does it just stop you seeing in? The first is a feature. The second is a flaw.

Care for included stones

Inclusions and healed fractures are planes of weakness, so an included crystal is structurally less forgiving than a clean one of the same species.

  • Never use an ultrasonic cleaner on a stone with a visible fluid inclusion or a significant fracture. Vibration propagates existing flaws, and an enhydro that loses its seal loses the water that made it interesting.
  • Avoid sudden temperature change. A trapped fluid expands and contracts at a different rate from the quartz around it. Do not take an included stone from cold outdoors into hot water.
  • Handle by the body, not the tip. Terminations are where the leverage is, and where chips happen.

Warm water, a soft brush and patience is the right routine for essentially all of them.
