Shungite: what it is and what it isn’t

Karelia · Carbon · Grades · The EMF question

Shungite is not a mineral. It is a rock — a carbon-rich, non-crystalline material found principally in Karelia in northwestern Russia, and named after the village of Shunga on Lake Onega. That distinction matters more than it sounds. Quartz has a fixed formula and a fixed structure. Shungite has neither, which is why its composition varies enormously from piece to piece and why grading it is genuinely difficult.

It is also extraordinarily old. The Karelian deposits date to the Paleoproterozoic, roughly two billion years, which puts them before the appearance of complex multicellular life. Whatever the carbon in shungite originally was, it predates almost everything we would recognise as living.

Where the carbon came from

This is still argued over. The leading explanation is biogenic: that the carbon is the residue of vast mats of primitive microorganisms and algae accumulating in an ancient basin, later buried and altered by heat and pressure. A competing view holds it to be abiogenic, deposited from carbon-bearing fluids or volcanic activity. The honest position is that the origin is not settled, and any seller who tells you flatly which it is has overstated the state of the science.

The grades, and what they actually mean

Shungite is usually sorted three ways by carbon content. The terminology is trade convention rather than a standard, so thresholds shift between sellers, but the broad divisions are consistent:

  • Elite, or noble, shungite. Roughly 90% carbon and up. Bright, silvery-black, almost metallic in lustre, and visibly different from the rest. It is also brittle and flaky — it cannot be carved or polished, which is why elite shungite is always sold as raw nuggets rather than shaped objects. It makes up a very small fraction of what is mined.
  • Middle grades, around 50–70% carbon. Less common in retail.
  • Type III, roughly 30–50% carbon. Matte black, structurally sound, and the only grade that takes a polish. Every shungite pyramid, sphere, tile and polished pendant you have seen is this. Nothing wrong with it, but if a listing offers a polished "elite" item, the terms are being used loosely.
A test that actually works

Shungite conducts electricity — unusual for a black rock, and the higher the carbon the better it conducts. A cheap multimeter or a simple battery-and-bulb circuit will show continuity across a genuine piece. Black plastic, resin, painted stone and polished black shale will not conduct. This is the single most reliable field test, and it works because of what shungite is rather than what it looks like.

Fullerenes

Fullerenes are hollow spherical molecules of pure carbon — the famous one, C₆₀, looks like a football. They were identified in the laboratory in 1985, work that won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1996, and were subsequently found to occur naturally in shungite. That is a genuinely interesting piece of mineralogy and it is why shungite appears in materials-science literature at all.

Two things get lost when this reaches marketing copy. Fullerenes are present in small quantities, not as a major constituent. And their presence in a rock does not transfer any of the properties studied in engineered fullerene materials to a pendant. The chemistry is real; the leap from chemistry to effect is not.

The EMF question

Shungite is very widely sold as protection against electromagnetic fields — phones, routers, laptops. We do not present that as established, and it is worth being clear about why rather than simply declining to comment.

Bulk carbon materials genuinely can attenuate radio-frequency energy, and carbon-loaded composites are used in engineered shielding. But shielding is a matter of geometry as much as material: it works by enclosing something. A pyramid on a desk or a stone on a cord does not enclose anything, and there is no peer-reviewed evidence that either measurably reduces exposure. Claims of health benefit from such reduction are a separate and even less supported step.

Buy shungite because it is a two-billion-year-old carbon rock that conducts electricity and contains naturally occurring fullerenes, which is remarkable enough. Treat the shielding claims as belief rather than specification.

Water and care

Shungite is also sold for water purification, on the general principle that carbon adsorbs organic compounds — which is how activated-carbon filters work. Shungite does have some adsorptive capacity. It is not a certified filter, it is not tested against any standard, and it should not be relied on to make questionable water safe.

For care: rinse type III if you like, but keep elite shungite dry. It is brittle and flaky, and repeated wetting accelerates crumbling. Expect any raw piece to leave black dust on hands and cloth at first; that settles with handling. Wash your hands after handling raw material, as with any fine mineral dust.

In the traditions

Peter the Great is said to have used the Karelian springs and given the stone its early reputation as a healing water. Modern crystal traditions treat shungite as the grounding and shielding stone — the one associated with absorbing what you would rather not carry. These are spiritual and folk associations, recorded here as such, and not physical or medical claims.
