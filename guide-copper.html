Why copper is wound around stones

The metal · The wrap · Tarnish · Green skin

Copper is one of a very small number of metals that occurs in nature as itself. Most metals are locked up in ores and have to be smelted out; copper turns up in riverbeds and rock seams as recognisable metal. That is why it was the first metal people worked, roughly ten thousand years ago, long before anyone could reach the temperatures iron needs.

It is also an outstanding conductor of heat and electricity — second only to silver among common metals, which is why the wiring in your walls is made of it. That fact is usually where the marketing starts, so it is worth being precise about what it does and does not imply. Copper conducts electricity. Whether a copper wire wound around a crystal does anything beyond hold the crystal is a separate question, and not one physics answers in the affirmative.

Why wrapping, rather than a setting

The practical reasons are good ones, and they have nothing to do with energy.

  • No drilling. A wrap holds a stone by tension and geometry. Drilling a hole through a crystal risks fracturing it and destroys any chance of resale as a specimen. Wrapping is completely reversible.
  • It fits irregular stones. A cast setting needs a predictable shape. Most of what gets wrapped — raw points, free-form tumbles, Herkimers, palm stones — is one-off geometry. Wire adapts to the individual stone.
  • Copper is soft enough to work cold. It sits around 3 on the Mohs scale and is highly malleable, so it can be bent, coiled and tightened by hand without a torch. It also work-hardens: the act of bending stiffens it, so a finished wrap is springier and holds better than the wire you started with.
  • It is cheap enough to be generous with. Silver at the same gauge and length costs many times more, which changes what a maker is willing to attempt.
What a good wrap looks like

Check that the stone does not shift when you press it, that wire ends are tucked inward rather than left proud where they can catch skin or fabric, and that the bail — the loop the chain runs through — is fully closed. A wrap that rattles will loosen further with wear.

Tarnish is not damage

Bare copper darkens. Left alone it goes from bright salmon-pink through brown to a deep near-black, and given years of damp it can develop the green film most people recognise from rooftops and statues. All of that is oxidation: copper reacting with oxygen, moisture and traces of sulfur in the air to form copper oxides and carbonates. The metal underneath is unchanged.

Whether you want it is taste. A great many makers deliberately patinate copper because the darkened metal reads as older and makes a pale stone stand out. If you would rather have it bright, the standard method is a mild acid — lemon juice, or a paste of vinegar and salt — followed by a thorough rinse and dry.

The catch is that this method uses both acid and salt, and those are exactly the two things that damage soft and porous stones. On a wrapped piece where you cannot isolate the metal from the stone, do not use it. A dry polishing cloth is slower and completely safe. If the stone is selenite, malachite, opal or anything you are unsure about, the cloth is the only correct answer.

Green skin, and why it happens

Copper jewellery sometimes leaves a green or blue-green mark on skin. This is not an allergy and not a sign of poor quality. Sweat is mildly acidic and contains salt; it reacts with the copper surface to form copper salts, and those salts are what transfer. It washes off with soap and water and stains nothing permanently.

How much it happens depends on your skin chemistry, the weather, and anything you have put on your hands — lotions and sunscreens accelerate it noticeably. If it bothers you, the usual fixes are a clear lacquer or jeweller's sealant on the inside surface, taking the piece off before exercise or showering, or wearing it as a pendant rather than a ring, where there is less friction and less sweat.

Genuine copper allergy exists but is rare. Contact dermatitis from costume jewellery is far more often a reaction to nickel, which is a common alloying metal — worth knowing if a piece is described as "copper-toned" or "copper-plated" rather than solid copper.

Buying notes

  • Solid copper or copper-plated? Plated wire wears through at stress points and exposes the base metal underneath. Solid copper wears too, but it wears into patina rather than into something else.
  • Gauge matters more than looks. Thin wire is easier to shape and easier to bend out of shape. For anything that will be worn daily, heavier gauge on the structural parts is worth having.
  • Ask about sealant. A sealed piece stays bright and will not mark skin, but the coating eventually wears and the piece then tarnishes unevenly. Unsealed is lower maintenance in the long run, if you like patina.
In the traditions

Copper is the metal of Venus in the alchemical scheme, and its name traces back through Latin cuprum to aes cyprium, "metal of Cyprus", where the Romans mined it. In crystal traditions it is the conductor and the joiner — the metal that carries between things rather than acting alone. That reputation is why so much of it is wound around stones rather than worn by itself. These are cultural associations with a long history, not physical properties.
