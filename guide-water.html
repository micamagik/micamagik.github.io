Which Crystals Should Never Go in Water?

What dissolves · What reacts · What absorbs

"Can I put this in water" has three different answers depending on the stone, and they fail in three completely different ways. Some minerals dissolve. Some react chemically and do not recover. Some are porous and drink it. Knowing which category a stone is in tells you everything you need.

The short version: quartz-family stones are fine, and almost everything else deserves a moment's thought.

Category one: stones that dissolve

Solubility is the most straightforward failure. The mineral is simply soluble in water, and contact removes material.

  • Selenite, satin spar, desert rose and gypsum flower are all the same mineral — gypsum, calcium sulfate dihydrate — and gypsum is slightly soluble. Running water dulls a polished surface within seconds. A soak will visibly etch it, and a long soak will round the edges off entirely. Selenite left in a damp bathroom will degrade over months without ever being deliberately wetted.
  • Halite is rock salt. It dissolves outright.
  • Calcite at Mohs 3 is not water-soluble in the ordinary sense but it is readily attacked by anything acidic — which includes rainwater, citrus, vinegar and many cleaning products. It will fizz in weak acid, which is the standard field test.
  • Ulexite and several borates are soluble enough to be damaged.

Category two: stones that react

These do not dissolve; they undergo a chemical change that does not reverse. This is the worst category, because the damage accumulates invisibly before it becomes obvious.

  • Pyrite oxidises in the presence of moisture and oxygen. The sulfide converts to sulfates, the surface develops rust-coloured staining and a faint sulfurous smell, and over time the specimen can crumble from the inside. Collectors call the process pyrite disease, and there is no cure once it is underway — only prevention, which means keeping the piece genuinely dry.
  • Malachite is a copper carbonate. Water dulls the polish and etches the surface. It is also toxic in powdered form, so wet-sanding or prolonged soaking is a bad idea for reasons beyond the stone.
  • Hematite on lower-grade or composite pieces can develop surface rust. Magnetic "hematite" is usually a manufactured ferrite and behaves differently again.
  • Anything wire-wrapped in copper or set in silver. The stone may be indifferent to water; the metal is not, and salt water in particular accelerates corrosion badly.

Category three: stones that absorb

Porous material takes liquid into itself rather than shedding it, and that changes both its appearance and what else it will absorb.

Ethiopian Welo opal is hydrophane, which literally means water-loving. Submerge one and it can turn translucent and lose its play-of-colour completely — a genuinely alarming thing to watch happen. It usually recovers as it dries, over hours or sometimes days, and the effect is normally reversible. But the same porosity means it will also permanently absorb oils, soap, lotion, perfume and dye. A Welo opal that goes into soapy water may come out with a haze that never leaves.

Australian opal is generally not hydrophane, but it is sensitive to sudden changes in temperature and humidity, which can produce fine surface cracking called crazing.

Turquoise is porous unless stabilised, and absorbs oils and dyes readily. Chrysocolla and much howlite behave similarly.

If you have already soaked something

Do not try to speed up drying. Air-dry at room temperature, away from direct sun and heat sources, and give it days rather than hours. Heat drives moisture out unevenly and is what causes crazing in opal. For a hydrophane opal that has gone cloudy, patience is genuinely the whole treatment.

What is actually safe

The quartz family is hard, chemically stable and effectively indifferent to water: clear quartz, amethyst, citrine, smoky quartz, rose quartz, agate, jasper, carnelian, tiger's eye, aventurine, prasiolite, Herkimer diamonds. Warm water and a soft brush is fine for all of them. So is obsidian, and so are most feldspars including labradorite, moonstone and amazonite — though these are softer at around 6 and prefer a gentle touch.

One exception within the safe list: any quartz with a large visible fluid inclusion or a significant internal fracture should stay out of temperature extremes, because the trapped fluid expands at a different rate from the surrounding stone.

Why salt makes everything worse

Salt water is the most commonly recommended cleaning method in crystal circles and one of the most destructive things you can do. Dry salt is abrasive enough to scratch anything below about 6 on the Mohs scale and will dull a polish. Dissolved salt accelerates metal corrosion, pits softer minerals, and works its way into porous stone where it recrystallises and expands. If a piece has any metal component, salt is simply off the table.

The default that is always safe

Dust with a dry, soft brush. Keep the stone out of direct sunlight. Do not soak it. That routine damages nothing, works for every mineral on this page, and is sufficient for almost everything you will own. Water is a convenience, not a requirement.
