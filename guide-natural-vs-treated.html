Natural, treated, dyed, coated

Heat · Dye · Irradiation · Coatings · Assembled stones

A great deal of the material on the crystal market has been altered in some way, and most of that alteration is entirely legitimate. Heating stones to improve colour is thousands of years old. The problem is never treatment; it is undisclosed treatment, and the price difference that silence conceals.

Here is what gets done, how to recognise it, and where it should be declared.

Heat

The most widespread treatment, and often permanent and stable.

  • Citrine. Natural citrine is genuinely uncommon. Most on the market is amethyst heated until its iron shifts colour. Heat-treated material tends toward a saturated orange-brown, frequently with white or cloudy zones near the base; natural citrine usually runs paler and smokier. Amethyst sold as "burnt" or with a strong orange hue at the tips has almost certainly been heated.
  • Prasiolite, green quartz, is nearly always heat-treated amethyst too.
  • Carnelian is routinely heated to deepen its orange, a practice documented in antiquity.
  • Ametrine, the purple-and-yellow banded quartz, occurs naturally in a single Bolivian deposit, and is otherwise produced by partial heating.

Irradiation

Exposure to radiation to alter colour centres, usually followed by heating to stabilise the result. Effectively all blue topaz is produced this way — natural blue topaz is very rare and very pale. The treated material is stable and is not radioactive when properly handled and released. Some smoky quartz on the market has also been irradiated rather than darkened naturally.

Dye

The treatment most often left undisclosed, because it is cheap and because it is applied to inexpensive material.

  • Agate is dyed in every colour sold. Bright, uniform blues, purples and greens in banded agate slices are dye. Look for colour pooling in the porous bands and running along cracks — dye follows structure, and natural colour rarely stops exactly at a fracture.
  • Howlite and magnesite are dyed blue and sold as turquoise. Howlite is naturally white with grey-black veining, and the veining stays visible under the dye.
  • Quartz is dyed to produce unnatural pinks and greens; the colour concentrates in fractures.
A quick dye check

Look at the stone under bright light with a loupe or a phone camera zoomed in. Dye concentrates where liquid could get in — cracks, porous zones, band boundaries. Natural colour follows crystal growth instead, which usually means zones and phantoms rather than fracture-tracing. A cotton bud with a little acetone on an unseen spot will sometimes lift surface dye, though it will do nothing to properly impregnated material.

Coatings

Aura quartz — angel aura, titanium aura, flame aura, opal aura — is natural quartz that has had a metal vapour bonded to its surface in a vacuum chamber. The crystal is real and the iridescence is manufactured. It is a modern industrial process, it is usually disclosed because the effect is unmistakable, and the coating can wear or scratch off since it sits on the surface rather than within the stone.

Filled, stabilised and reconstituted

  • Stabilised means porous stone impregnated with resin to make it hard enough to cut and polish. The majority of commercial turquoise is stabilised, and this is standard, accepted practice.
  • Reconstituted means ground stone mixed with binder and pressed back into a solid. It contains the mineral but is substantially resin. Far less valuable, and it should always be stated.
  • Fracture-filled stones have had cracks infiltrated with resin or glass to improve apparent clarity. Look for flash of an odd colour in the fracture as you tilt the stone.

Assembled stones

A doublet is a thin slice of stone glued to a dark backing; a triplet adds a clear cap over the top. Both are common with opal, where they let very thin precious material be used at all. They are legitimate products and much cheaper than solid stone — but they are not solid stone, they can separate if soaked, and the difference should be on the listing. Viewed from the side, the glue line is usually visible as a straight boundary that no natural stone would have.

Outright imitations

Not treatments but substitutions, sold under names that sound mineral: goldstone and blue goldstone are glass with copper or cobalt inclusions; opalite is glass; cherry quartz is usually glass. Glass gives itself away with perfectly round gas bubbles, swirl patterns, warmth to the touch compared with quartz, and colour too even to be natural.

How to ask

One question covers it: "Has this been heated, dyed, irradiated, coated, stabilised or filled?" A seller who knows their material will answer plainly, and often volunteers it first. Hesitation or vagueness on a species where treatment is routine is itself an answer.

None of this should put you off treated stones. A heat-treated citrine is a real quartz crystal and can be beautiful. Just pay treated prices for treated material — the gap between that and untreated is frequently several times over.
